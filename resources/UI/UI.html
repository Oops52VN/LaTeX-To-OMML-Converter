INTERNATIONAL STANDARD ISO/IEC 80000

Quantities and units — Comprehensive Specification for Natural Sciences and Technology

The international standard ISO 80000, jointly produced by the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission, provides universal specifications for quantities, units, symbols, and mathematical styling in scientific publications. Its primary purpose is to eliminate ambiguity across disciplines, ensure semantic clarity, and guarantee precise interchange of scientific data across digital platforms.

Part 1 covers general principles, defining the mathematical nature of physical quantities, the architecture of coherent systems of units, and foundational typographical rules. Part 2 standardizes mathematical signs, logic, operators, intervals, functions, coordinates, vectors, and matrices.

Clause 4: Variables, Functions and Operators

Normative verbatim text excerpted from ISO 80000-2:2019, Clause 4

It is customary to use different sorts of letters for different sorts of entities, for example x and y for numbers or elements of some given set, f and g for functions, and so forth. This custom makes mathematical formulas far more readable and assists fundamentally in setting up an unambiguous context.

Variables such as x and y, as well as running numbers such as the index i in the summation of xi, are printed in sloping or italic type.

Parameters such as a and b, which may be considered as constant in a particular context, are likewise printed in sloping or italic type.

The same italic convention applies to functions in general, such as f and g.

An explicitly defined function that does not depend on the shifting context of the text is printed in upright or Roman type, such as sin, cos, tan, exp, ln, lg, and the gamma function Gamma.

Mathematical constants whose numerical values never alter are printed in upright or Roman type, such as the base of natural logarithms e equal to approximately 2.718281828, the circle constant pi equal to approximately 3.141592653, and the imaginary unit i satisfying i squared equals minus one.

Well-defined mathematical operators are also printed in upright or Roman type, including the divergence operator div, the variation operator delta in delta x, and the differential operator d in df divided by dx.

Numbers expressed in the form of digits are always printed in upright or Roman type, for instance 351204, 1.32, and 7/8.

Physical Quantities, Numerical Values and Representations

Foundational definitions governed by ISO 80000-1, Clause 3 and Clause 7

A physical quantity is defined as a property of a phenomenon, body, or substance that can be expressed quantitatively as a number together with an appropriate reference. The value of a quantity Q is formally expressed as the mathematical product of its pure numerical value {Q} and its measurement unit [Q], written as Q = {Q} · [Q]. For instance, a mass m of twenty-five kilograms is expressed as the product of the numerical scalar twenty-five and the unit kilogram.

Symbols representing physical quantities are strictly restricted to single letters from the Latin or Greek alphabet, printed without exception in sloping or italic type. When subscripts or superscripts are appended, the italic typeface of the principal letter remains mandatory regardless of whether the surrounding prose is set in Roman, bold, or italic fonts.

Products of quantity symbols are indicated either through adjacent placement, with a thin intervening space, with a centered half-high dot, or with a cross multiplication sign. Division between quantities is represented by a solidus fraction slash, by a horizontal bar, or through negative integer exponents. When a solidus is employed, parentheses must be inserted whenever consecutive operations would introduce ambiguity.

Printing Rules and Typographic Requirements for Units

Normative conventions for International System of Units symbols (ISO 80000-1, Clause 7)

Unit symbols must always be printed in upright or Roman type. Under no circumstance may an italic font be applied to a unit of measurement, ensuring immediate visual distinction between a quantity symbol such as m representing mass and a unit symbol m representing the meter.

Lower-case letters are used for unit symbols generally, exemplified by m for meter, s for second, and kg for kilogram. However, whenever the official name of a unit is derived from the proper name of an individual, the initial character of its symbol is capitalized. Prominent instances include N for newton, J for joule, W for watt, Pa for pascal, V for volt, A for ampere, and Hz for hertz. When the complete name of such a unit is written out in full in running text, it begins with an ordinary lower-case letter, as in five newtons or ten joules.

A non-breaking space or small thin space is strictly required between the numerical scalar and the accompanying unit symbol, preserving the integrity of expressions such as 25 kg, 100 m, and 20 °C. The scalar and unit must never be joined without spacing. Unit symbols remain invariant under grammatical pluralization; they never take an appended letter s, meaning fifty kilograms is written as 50 kg rather than 50 kgs. Unit symbols are never concluded with a period unless positioned at the natural conclusion of a sentence.

Numbers, Digit Grouping and Decimal Markers

Standardized representation of numerical values (ISO 80000-1, Clause 7.3)

Numbers expressed as numerical digits are printed in upright or Roman type. To facilitate effortless scanning of long numerical sequences, numbers containing four or more digits on either side of the decimal marker may be grouped in sets of three, counting leftward and rightward from the decimal point.

The separation between these triads must be effected solely by a small thin space. The employment of commas, periods, or apostrophes as thousands separators is explicitly prohibited by the standard because commas and periods serve as decimal markers in various international jurisdictions. The standard example of a grouped sequence is 12 345 678.901 23.

The decimal marker may be rendered either as a comma on the baseline or as a raised or baseline dot. Within an individual document, absolute consistency must be preserved, and the chosen marker must never be confused with digit group separation.

Typographic Roles of Subscripts and Superscripts

Semantic classification of indices (ISO 80000-2, Clause 4)

The typographical presentation of subscripts and superscripts in scientific formulas conveys critical semantic significance. The standard establishes an unequivocal dichotomy between indices that represent mathematical variables and indices that represent descriptive words or constants.

A subscript is set in sloping or italic type if and only if it represents a physical quantity, an algebraic variable, or a running index. Common examples include the constant-pressure heat capacity Cp where the subscript p denotes the variable quantity pressure, the array element xi where i represents an integer running index, and the molar volume Vm where m designates the molar quantity variable.

Conversely, a subscript is set in upright or Roman type whenever it represents a descriptive word, a standard textual abbreviation, a chemical compound identifier, or an invariant number. Standard instances include the kinetic energy Ek where k abbreviates kinetic, the standard acceleration of gravity gn where n stands for normal, the maximum threshold xmax where max abbreviates maximum, and half-life duration T1/2 where one half is a fixed numerical fraction.

Calculus, Differentials and Integral Notations

Mathematical operators and measures of integration (ISO 80000-2, Clause 11)

In the field of mathematical analysis, operators designate operational transformations and must be typographically separated from algebraic variable names. The differential operator d utilized in total derivatives, differential forms, and measures of integration is printed in upright or Roman type.

Setting the differential operator in upright type eliminates the ambiguity between the differential product d multiplied by x and the infinitesimal increment dx. In integration, the differential element is separated from the preceding integrand by a small thin space, producing the standard formulation of the integral of f(x) dx.

The partial derivative operator round d is printed in upright type as specified in the mathematical symbol tables of ISO 80000-2, reflecting its fixed operational identity across all coordinate branches.

Scalars, Vectors, Matrices and Tensors

Multidimensional entities and tensor notation (ISO 80000-2, Clause 15)

Scalar quantities are designated by individual letters printed in sloping or italic lightface type, exemplified by a, b, x, y, and the temperature T.

Vector quantities are printed in boldface sloping or italic type, or alternatively in lightface italic surmounted by an arrow accent. The bold italic designation is preferred in modern mathematical and physical typography, illustrated by the velocity vector v, the displacement vector r, and the force vector F.

Matrices, second-order tensors, and linear transformations are designated by uppercase boldface letters set in upright or Roman type, such as matrix A, identity matrix I, and stress tensor T. Alternatively, sans-serif bold sloping letters may be adopted provided that the chosen convention is maintained consistently throughout the work.

Standard Number Sets and Intervals

Set theory notations and domain boundaries (ISO 80000-2, Clause 6)

Standard sets of numbers are printed in blackboard bold or bold Roman typeface. The letter N designates the set of natural numbers, the letter Z denotes the set of integers, the letter Q represents the set of rational numbers, the letter R symbolizes the field of real numbers, and the letter C designates the field of complex numbers.

Numerical intervals along the real line are demarcated by square brackets and round parentheses. A closed interval containing both endpoints a and b is designated by square brackets as [a, b]. An open interval excluding both endpoints is denoted either by round parentheses as (a, b) or by inverted square brackets as ]a, b[. Half-open intervals are correspondingly indicated by combining the appropriate bracket styles, for instance [a, b) or [a, b[ for an interval including the left boundary while excluding the right.

Standard Notation Comparative Reference

Systematic comparison between ISO 80000 specifications and deprecated conventions

The following overview synthesizes the normative typographical requirements of ISO 80000 alongside common legacy habits that must be avoided in modern technical typesetting:

In exponential expressions, Euler number e must be set in upright Roman type rather than sloping italic, because e represents a universal constant rather than an arbitrary base variable.

The imaginary unit i or j must be set in upright Roman type to avoid confusion with running integer indices.

The differential operator d in derivatives and integrals must be set in upright Roman type, avoiding the appearance of an algebraic variable product.

Measurement units must be printed in upright Roman type and separated from numerical values by a mandatory thin space, eliminating joined representations like 25kg.

Vector symbols must be rendered in boldface italic rather than plain lightface italic, clearly distinguishing vector entities from scalar magnitudes.
